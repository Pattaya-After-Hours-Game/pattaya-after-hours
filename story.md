# Pattaya After Hours: From One Bar to the Whole Soi

This is the story reference for the game: the premise, recurring cast, chapter
arc and tone to preserve when adding content. The chapter and venue names match
[scripts/street_content.gd](scripts/street_content.gd). Current progression gates
are documented below; future hooks are ideas, not shipped quests.

## You came here to relax

You and Anong have taken over **The Blue Hour**, a tired beer bar with six stools,
a warm fridge and a battered neon sign.

The previous owner said it was a goldmine. He also said the roof didn't leak.

Your first ambition is modest: keep the drinks cold and put more money in the
till than you take out. Eventually, you will own a street of beer bars, hostess
venues and gogos, each with its own customers, staff and problems.

> **Anong:** “The last owner said this place practically runs itself. He's at the
> airport. You're on ice duty.”

The central joke is that you came here to relax. Now you manage forty people,
seven fridges and a monster's bar tab. That is the story's comic ambition, not a
required headcount or equipment objective.

## What kind of story this is

Cheeky adult nightlife comedy starts with familiar business problems and grows
into cartoon chaos. A warm fridge matters before a giant lizard does. Regulars
argue about pool rules, suppliers explain why the delivery is late, and your
rival orders another LED screen instead of fixing his bar.

The player earns a place in a neighbourhood. Growth means customers returning,
staff taking responsibility and neighbours remembering help. It should feel good
to make a small beer bar work, even when a gogo is available elsewhere.

The joke usually lands on the owner's optimism, overcomplicated promotions or
impossibly bad timing. Characters have ambitions beyond the player's business.
Pattaya informs the setting; venues and people are fictional. All staff and
performers are adults. Private activity is implied and off-screen. Hosts can
decline bar-fine requests, and accepting a request changes availability rather
than removing their agency.

“Rent is due on Tuesday” expresses the opening pressure. It is not a mandatory
real-time deadline or a separate Tuesday rent mechanic in the live economy.

## Same beer, different night

The Blue Hour charges ฿80 for a standard drink. A Hostess bar charges ฿100;
a gogo charges ฿120. The extra twenty baht belongs to the kind of night on offer,
not an automatic price rise every time the owner buys new chairs.

> **Anong:** “Eighty here. A hundred down the soi. A hundred and twenty under
> the spotlights. Bo still wants his delivery paid.”

Bo's ordinary supply starts at ฿38 per drink, reducing to ฿30 with the best
agreement. A fresh Blue Hour opens with twelve drinks and ฿228 for a full
six-pack. Existing owners keep their till and fridge. His emergency consignment
remains a subsidised ฿14-per-sale favour, not an ordinary wholesale deal.

## A reason to stay for another round

After the first renovation, Anong notices an awkward quiet patch: the bar looks
better, but customers finish the visit they came for and leave. She starts the
**Regulars Club**, a place to settle in rather than a discount on the beer.

> **Anong:** “You bought them comfortable chairs. Give them a reason to stay in them.”

At stage two, ฿600/1,200/2,400 club investments and 10/20/35 local regulars
invite 35%/65%/100% of new visitors to plan and budget for one extra drink,
up to three. Existing guests keep the visit they arrived for. The birthday
still stops at three rounds; nobody gets a fourth because of a membership card.

Pim's better sound system costs ฿2,400 after the original ฿1,200 music purchase,
bringing repeat orders from 25 to 18 seconds. Bartenders can learn 12-second,
then 10-second service at stage two for ฿1,200 and ฿1,600. The extra skill stays
with the person, even after a contract ends. These are business purchases,
not additional character quests or automatic rewards.

The first management appointment now costs ฿8,000. Dao can bring in the existing
฿10 per open second sooner, while the owner chooses between better visits,
faster service and the next building. Anong still insists on stock and payroll
reserves: an empty fridge cannot serve an encore.

## The next problem is a better problem

The Blue Hour's progress is a repeating business comedy. Add seats and discover
that Dao cannot pour quickly enough. Train the team and discover an empty room.
Improve the frontage and discover a queue outside. Now those extra seats make
sense again. Anong calls this progress. Bo calls it another delivery.

> **Anong:** “Yesterday we needed customers. Today we need chairs. Tomorrow you
> will tell me we need a second pair of your hands.”

Visitors arrive with one or two rounds in mind. Locals are more likely to settle
in; tourists may stop for one. A club invitation or birthday adds one round, not
an unlimited tab. They finish the final drink, finish pool if necessary, and go.
Even someone who only came for one can leave happy and become a regular.

Pim's music speeds the rhythm of drinking and frees seats sooner. Across the
three beer-bar stages, owners can buy seven seating tiers, five frontage tiers,
four fridges, four music tiers and six personal service-training tiers. Each
stage opens more options; none installs itself. Other venue types retain their
existing investments while sharing the new visitor behaviour.

Service's profit report gives Anong evidence: actual arrivals, orders, drinks and
completed visits, with guaranteed manager income shown separately. Her advice
points to the constraint the last shift exposed. These are working business
systems, not new personal quests or promises of an endless customer stream.

## A rich street still needs a local following

> **Anong:** “The Blue Hour can buy the paint. It cannot buy Neon Mango's regulars.”

The shared till can fund a renovation, but each venue earns its own happy
visits, regulars and healthy shifts. Manager takings make expansion affordable;
they do not prove that the new room serves its customers well. The renovation
in Upgrades shows that venue's progress and what still needs work. The cards
below it show what the next stage will let you buy.

## A shift has a rhythm

The owner can pour a drink, step outside to welcome passersby, or reassure a guest
who is about to leave. Pouring and welcoming begin with ten-second recovery;
practice and equipment improve that rhythm. A chat buys ten seconds of patience,
not a guaranteed sale or a new friendship. The bell means a real order is waiting.

The crowd includes familiar faces and visitors with different clothes and looks.
Their appearance stays with them throughout a visit; it does not decide their
budget or behaviour. A better bar changes in visible ways: buying fans installs
fans, and buying a sports screen gives the regulars a match to argue over.
Renovating alone does not give away the equipment; fitted beer-bar screens
stay dark until the sports upgrade is bought. Compact benches make room for
twelve people without losing the pool table or fans. Bo starts with six-packs, then offers twelve-drink deliveries at a cheaper unit
price. “Almost enough room in the fridge” is still not a delivery agreement.
Better refrigeration earns its reputation through satisfied visits: colder beer
gives the regulars something good to tell their friends.

Nida, 28, is the adult promoter at the entrance: friendly, practical and fond of
music nights. She welcomes passersby while the owner pours. Her flat-fee ten-day contract
with no drink commission makes the partnership a business choice. She can bring
people through the door; she cannot rescue a queue that nobody serves. Training
lets her take on a busier frontage as the venue grows.

> **Anong:** “She can get them through the door. You still have to get them a drink.”

Nida faces the street to welcome passersby. Pim’s purchasable music system
brings the next round forward; the bartender still has to pour it. Auto-serve
buys the owner relief from constant tapping, not extra time on the clock.
Dao’s management tiers bring predictable income while the venue trades, alongside
the ordinary customers’ tabs. Better supplier agreements make future deliveries
cheaper; Bo does not refund yesterday’s fridge. The first bartender now costs
the same upfront as Nida, making a colleague within reach sooner. They still need
wages and a full queue to be useful. Nida handles the doorway while on duty;
practising the owner’s welcome only helps when she takes time off.

This is her playable role, not a new scripted quest. She leaves her post when
off duty or out of contract, and keeps her training if she returns.

The colleague behind the counter is now visibly doing real work. Ready staff
serve an actual waiting order, then take their 25/20/15-second recovery (12/10 after stage-two training). The owner
clearing a queue no longer sends them back to the beginning of a warmup. Their
serving pose and the Service tally confirm what they sold. The top-right
portrait list shows who is ready and who is recovering. When the
fridge is empty, the indicator says so instead of pretending someone is pouring.
At ฿3,000, owner auto-serve is a separate convenience for the owner's tapping;
it does not replace the colleague's own clock or create buyers. Quiet orders
belong to the bartender. Owner automation steps in when the queue outgrows the
on-duty team, or a guest would leave before anyone recovers. Without a bartender,
the owner covers normal service. Nida's return is a clear contract renewal at the
door: her skills stay, and Anong insists on keeping payroll and a delivery in
reserve after paying her fee.

> **Anong:** “She's ready. Give her an order, not another stopwatch.”

## May has a better story

May remembers which guest wanted to talk, which wanted karaoke and which still
insists the pool table slopes. Fah turns a song request into a small event.
A guest can offer to buy a host a drink, and the owner can match the company
to the conversation.

> **Anong:** “She sold a drink and settled an argument. I was only going to do one of those.”

Someone must pour stocked lager and the guest must afford the quote. A topic
match earns ฿40 more. Twenty seconds of company keeps the host's appeal in the
room; three completed rounds start a brief Room Buzz. A big booking fee is
tempting, but a lively bar has its own value. May and Fah keep their preferences,
and training makes their hosted drinks worth more too.

## A booking is a trade-off

At a Hostess bar, a customer can ask and a willing adult host can accept. The
owner sees the decision plainly: ฿500 for the bar, thirty seconds away, and less
appeal while the colleague is absent. A visible countdown brings her back;
there is no private scene. May and Fah still prefer hosted drinks.

> **Anong:** “Count the fee. Then count who is still here to welcome the next guest.”

Host development earns stronger appeal and higher future fees: ฿750, then
฿1,000. Training belongs to the colleague, not the contract. Paid auto-accept
handles the same eligible requests while the street is live; it cannot change
anyone's preferences or invent a customer who can afford the booking.

## Something to look forward to: Anong's birthday

By day six, the Blue Hour should feel like a place the player knows. The next
reason to open is personal: **Anong's Birthday on day seven**. From the start,
Events lists the date; two game days beforehand the street banner and Anong's
reminder ask the owner to fill the fridge and sort the rota.

> **Anong:** “No presents. Bring your friends. And please, this year, cold beer.”

The invitations and bunting cost nothing. From noon until the normal 1am close,
the street gets more visitors and the Blue Hour is the gathering place. Guests
plan an extra round, give the team more patience and may buy a third drink.
Pim's purchased music brings the next round forward again. Nobody conjures
stock, customers still have budgets, and serving the room earns the night's money.
A crowded party can expose a slow bar; Chat, a colleague and a prepared fridge
still matter. Nida works for her usual ten-day contract fee; there is no drink commission or birthday surcharge.

At closing, the player sees the actual party trading: visitors, drinks, costs,
contribution, new regulars and people lost to queues. There is no extra envelope
of money for opening the report. Good service leaves something that lasts: people
who want to return, and the ordinary happy-visit milestones toward renovation.

The next dates are **the Regulars' Reunion (day fourteen)**, **Pim's Mixtape Party
(day twenty-one)** and **Bo's Thank-You Night (day twenty-eight)**. Those three
neighbourhood gatherings rotate afterward. Anong does not age a year every week.
These parties use the same bounded traffic and real orders, alongside the wider
street's unpredictable events. New random incidents wait until after the party;
already-announced incidents keep their promises.

The calendar belongs to the campaign. Putting the game away does not use up the
party, require a real-world appointment or break a streak. Existing campaigns
past the first birthday get the next scheduled gathering without a cash handout.

## The coins under the till

At closing, Anong finds the jar the previous owner labelled “miscellaneous”.
It turns out to contain money, not another set of keys that fits nothing.

> **Anong:** “Ten baht from someone you actually served. Count the customers,
> not how many times you pressed the button. Tomorrow needs cold beer too.”

Each served visit adds ฿10 to the house tip jar, counted once when the street
finishes its night. Another round from the same guest does not add another tip.
The closing countdown shows the money counted; Service and Journal keep the
receipt. These are actual live customers, not an offline attendance bonus.

If the fridge empties first, Bo offers six drinks on consignment: he takes ฿14
back when each one sells. Anong covers two minutes of overhead while the owner
serves and the staff take a break. The jar helps tomorrow; Bo's recovery delivery
gets tonight moving again. Neither prints customers or gives away the stock.

## Noon, last orders and tomorrow's mop

The playable day now has a clock: noon opening, 1am closing, and an optional
extension to 3am chosen separately for each venue each night. One real second
is one game minute. The visible DAY counter follows the campaign from Day 1,
advancing at midnight. Anong's short business reports are not whole calendar days.

The street gets busy in waves: beer-bar peaks run from 5–9pm, Hostess bars from
7–11pm, and gogos from 10pm–2am. The extra attraction still competes for real
passersby. More trade also leaves more work: each drink sold during peak time
makes twice the mess. A gogo's final peak hour tempts the owner into late opening.

Late takings come with attention. Operating after 1am raises heat; a raid fines
the offending venue and shuts it until noon. This is separate from the warned
street inspection event. Losing the gamble leaves a recoverable business,
not confiscated staff or an ending screen.

> **Anong:** “That extra round came with paperwork.”

Closed hours bring ordinary work back into the story: cleaning, buying stock,
and checking the rota. Fifteen seconds of nightly clean-up clears the dirt left by
actual drink sales. Anong calls for the mop, then the next opening; the owner
can choose to stay and finish preparing. The owner can
skip to noon without finishing, but an unclean bar attracts fewer customers and
an empty fridge stays empty. Bo's delivery still costs money.

> **Anong:** “You can skip to opening. The floor can't.”

Staff agree to ten-day contracts. Paying for training develops a person, so those
skills stay with them if they leave and later return. New bartenders need the
owner's help with a rush; learning the job gradually buys breathing room.

> **Anong:** “The contract ends. Knowing how to pour a drink doesn't.”

These are current source mechanics. Personal staff quests and other future
hooks below remain separate ideas.

## Anong keeps the next step in view

Her plan is now visible near the top of Service. It starts with ten actual drinks
and someone returning, then follows the earned renovation, the reopening and
handing Dao the keys. Later it points toward a second address, trusted managers,
Anton's rival showroom and the street festival. The figures come from the real
business, and the button takes the owner to the relevant controls.

These are reminders of the existing story, not extra quests or rewards. Anong
never asks for a login streak or pays the same congratulations twice. She keeps
an ambition visible while the player chooses equipment, staff and promotions.

Training is a smaller investment in a colleague; the first manager is within
reach sooner. Final renovations still need hundreds of satisfied visits and a
local following, but the player spends less time with a finished business waiting
for one more number. No ordinary drinks become more expensive because the bar
has a new sign.

## The first story arc

### Chapter 1 — Rent Is Due on Tuesday

Rescue the Blue Hour. The owner’s Serve and Welcome actions recover over ten seconds, with local
upgrades making them faster. A quick chat can buy an impatient guest time, at the
cost of delaying the next welcome. Reassurance still needs a fulfilled order.

The first successes are tangible: a served order, cold
stock, a returning customer and a quiet shift that still pays its way. Pool and
small equipment purchases give the bar a personality before a major renovation.

The reopening is your first public declaration that this place might last. The
new sign works—then the party threatens to trip the power. Bo's generator offer
suddenly sounds less ridiculous. Hiring and appointing a manager turn survival
into a business that can operate without your hand on every bottle.

### Chapter 2 — Everybody Knows Your Bar

The regulars recognise you. So do the suppliers and rivals. Anong begins looking
past the Blue Hour's doorway. Another owner announces his retirement for the
fourth time, creating the opportunity to buy a hostess bar. Its ฿24,000 base
lease sits between a ฿12,000 beer bar and a ฿60,000 gogo. The owner's retirement
is a story opportunity, not a hidden permanent discount. Every fortnight the
property board advertises a one-day 10% retirement offer, rotating venue types.
Higher running costs and a new customer base still need work.

The second lease is not a second guaranteed crowd. It needs its own reputation
with customers. The player learns that financing a building and establishing a
business are different jobs. A second beer bar is also a valid purchase here.

### Chapter 3 — Upstairs, Downstairs, Everywhere

The story shifts from serving everyone personally to trusting other people.
Dao takes responsibility, Pim thinks about the music, and Mali wants promotions
that do more than leave someone else with the cleaning.

A Hostess bar adds hosted nightlife, actual customer requests and staff availability
to the decisions. Private activity stays behind the door. Two managed venues are
the milestone: the street is becoming an organisation rather than one frantic
owner running between fridges.

### Chapter 4 — The Neon Arms Race

A first gogo brings a stage, performers and higher running costs. Anton sends
flowers; the card somehow mentions the dimensions of his LED screen.

Performances, premium orders and opening-night crowds raise the stakes. More
visitors can expose weak service just as easily as they improve takings. Pim's
headline booking and Anton's conveniently timed re-reopening establish the
rivalry as a recurring part of life on the street.

### Chapter 5 — The Whole Soi Knows Your Name

The player has built a mixed street rather than discarded every small business
on the way to the largest one. Beer bars, Hostess bars and gogos each have a role.
Neighbours, managers and regulars give the district its character.

The **After Hours Street Festival** brings the first arc together: a crowd,
shared preparations and a headline guest with dorsal plates. Serve Godzilla's
giant drinks and he becomes a sleeping tourist attraction. Fail and the street
has repairs to make, but the business and story continue.

> **Anong:** “You came here to relax. Now look at this place. Same time next year?”

### After the festival — The Soi Never Sleeps

The first festival finishes an arc, not the campaign. Venues keep trading,
events return and the player can host the festival again. There is no forced
one-year ending. New seasons and character stories belong within this continuing
street; they should not reset away the value of established venues.

## Writing the next update

Keep Anong a co-owner with opinions. Give colleagues a reason to want
responsibility. Let Anton occasionally create an opportunity by overspending.
Use Bo's excuses sparingly enough that an ordinary on-time delivery can be funny.
Let customers remember good service, and let failure lead to another shift.

Future hooks, not implemented quests:

- June's cafe fund becomes a neighbourhood collaboration rather than a purchase
  of her loyalty.
- Pim attempts a first original-music night with a budget the business can afford.
- A pool league makes rival bars cooperate over fixtures while arguing about rules.
- A regular's “investment” becomes a running joke with an actual written proposal.
- The next festival's committee insists that monster-sized glasses are a normal
  procurement category now.

When a hook becomes playable, update this file and the relevant content/model
code together. Keep the economic consequences visible, the adult activity
non-explicit, and the ordinary people more important than the spectacle.

## Closing clean-up and tomorrow's opening

After the last venue closes for the night, a free **15-second clean-up** starts
for all dirty venues, including those without a manager. A visible street button
counts down to noon. **Stay here** cancels the automatic jump for that night;
cleaning continues, and **Skip to 12pm** remains available when you are ready.
Even an already-clean street gives you the full fifteen-second choice.

The countdown waits for late-trading venues to finish. Daytime closure and a
temporary event closure cannot end an otherwise scheduled late night. It advances
only during foreground street play, never on the start menu or through offline
estimates; its countdown pauses while the street is hidden by district/arcade
views. Normal clock rules still apply. Schema 10 preserves the remaining time
and cancellation across reloads; older saves start with no pending countdown.

At zero, completed cleaning is retained and the existing skip moves the clock
to noon. Stock is not bought, no income is awarded, and contracts age through the
skipped hours as before. Owner-closed or cash-shortage-closed venues stay closed.
The date already advanced at midnight, so the jump does not add another day.

## One less cut of the till

> **Anong:** “Nida has her contract. The drinks stay in the till. And tell the
